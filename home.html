The budget story

Start with the problem, then follow the public dollars.

A useful budget should explain more than what will be spent. It should show why action is needed, who is affected, what government can realistically do, and how the public will know whether the plan worked. This page is your guide to those answers.

Challenges facing the County and community

Growth changes the scale of every service.

These are not one-year issues. They are identified through population and development trends, service demand, asset condition, departmental operating data, emergency experience, public input, and the multi-year capital planning process.

01

Growth and service capacity

More residents, visitors, homes, and businesses increase demand for roads, public safety, parks, permitting, emergency response, and everyday County operations.

Root issue
Demand can grow faster than facilities, staffing, and infrastructure capacity.
Time horizon
Annual service delivery with multi-year capital needs.
02

Infrastructure and resilience

Roads, drainage, public facilities, coastal assets, and utilities must be maintained while new capacity is added and the County remains prepared for severe weather.

Root issue
Age, use, growth, geography, and storm exposure create overlapping needs.
Time horizon
Projects often extend beyond a single budget year.
03

Workforce and service continuity

Reliable public service depends on recruiting, retaining, and equipping employees in a competitive labor market while managing salaries, benefits, and retirement costs.

Root issue
Turnover, specialized skills, workload, and compensation pressures affect capacity.
Time horizon
Immediate vacancies and long-term workforce planning.
04

Uneven needs across the community

Challenges are not uniform. Coastal visitation, rural distance, development patterns, transportation access, and service availability affect communities differently—and often involve state, municipal, and special-district partners.

Root issue
One County contains distinct places, populations, and demand patterns.
Time horizon
Near-term response informed by long-term geographic change.

What is new? The underlying responsibilities are ongoing; their scale, location, cost, and urgency change over time. The budget therefore combines immediate operating decisions with longer-range capital and financial planning.

How this budget responds

Fund today’s services. Build tomorrow’s capacity.

The budget cannot eliminate every challenge in one year. Its role is to maintain essential services, address the most actionable needs, reduce future risk, and sequence larger solutions at a scale the County can deliver and sustain.

Are results expected to improve?

Some results arrive this year. Others require sustained progress.

Success is not measured by spending the appropriation. It is measured by whether services remain dependable, projects reach milestones, planned work is completed, and the public sees improved capacity or a slower rate of service degradation.

During FY 2027

Track delivery

Workload, response, participation, staffing, financial performance, and project milestones show whether implementation is on course.

At year-end

Compare plan to result

Actual spending, completed work, service measures, staffing changes, and capital progress can be compared with the adopted plan.

Across future budgets

Judge sustained impact

Long-term challenges require trend data. The County can adjust strategies as demand, cost, condition, and outcomes change.

What the public gets

How much government costs

The budget is shared—but it is not one bill.

County services are funded through a mix of property and sales taxes, state-shared and intergovernmental revenue, service charges, permits, restricted revenues, and available balances. Visitors and businesses contribute alongside residents.

Different services use different funding

Property taxes support broad governmental services. Sales and tourism-related taxes share cost with visitors. Fees connect some costs to a specific service or activity.
